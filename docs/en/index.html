What it is
A series of Marian apparitions reported by Catherine Labouré, then a novice of the Daughters of Charity, at the chapel of the motherhouse at 140 rue du Bac, Paris, between July and December 1830 — and the devotional medal (popularly the 'Miraculous Medal') struck in 1832 on the basis of her account. In Portuguese and Spanish the image on the medal is widely venerated as Nossa Senhora das Graças / Nuestra Señora de las Gracias (Our Lady of Grace).[1] [8]
Attestation chain
The account of the apparitions rests on Catherine Labouré's testimony as relayed by her confessor Jean-Marie Aladel CM, and on her own later written accounts. She remained publicly anonymous as the seer during her lifetime and refused to appear before the 1836 diocesan inquiry; her identity became generally known only around her death in 1876.[12] [6] [10]
Church approval status
Approval attached primarily to the medal and its cult, not to a formal declaratory sentence on the apparitions: Archbishop de Quélen of Paris authorized the medal (struck June 1832); his 1836 diocesan inquiry gathered favorable evidence; Leo XIII instituted a liturgical feast (27 November) in 1894; Pius XII canonized Catherine Labouré in 1947. The French Wikipedia synthesis notes the apparitions were never declared authentic at the close of a completed canonical trial, while devotional accounts state the 1836 inquiry pronounced the medal of supernatural origin — this dataset records both readings (see the 1836 event).[12] [10] [11] [8]
Place
Chapel of Our Lady of the Miraculous Medal, motherhouse of the Daughters of Charity, 140 rue du Bac, 7th arrondissement, Paris.[1]
Feast
27 November — feast of the Manifestation of the Immaculate Virgin under the title of the Miraculous Medal, instituted by Leo XIII on 23 July 1894.[8]

1830 1830-04 ? Paris, France Catherine Labouré enters the seminary of the Daughters of Charity at the rue du Bac — Having begun her postulancy at Châtillon in January 1830, Catherine arrives about three months later at the seminary (novitiate) of the motherhouse, 140 rue du Bac, Paris. The chapel's account gives the sequence but no exact arrival day.[6]
1830 1830-07-18 Paris, France First reported apparition (night of 18–19 July 1830) — Catherine Labouré later recounted being awakened at about 11:30 p.m. and led to the chapel, where she reported a roughly two-hour conversation with the Virgin Mary, including warnings of coming calamities in France. The account is known through what she told her confessor Jean-Marie Aladel and through her own later written accounts; she remained anonymous as the seer during her lifetime.[2] [12]
1830 1830-11-27 Paris, France Second reported apparition — the medal vision — During the novices' evening prayer at about 5:30 p.m., Catherine reported seeing the Virgin standing on a globe, rays streaming from her hands, framed by the invocation 'O Mary conceived without sin, pray for us who have recourse to you', then a reverse design with the letter M, a cross, and two hearts — with the request, as she reported it: 'Have a medal struck on this model; those who wear it will receive great graces.'[3] [12]
1830 1830-12 ? Paris, France Third reported apparition (December 1830) — Catherine reported a final apparition in December 1830, repeating the medal vision. The exact day was not recorded — only the month is known, as the French Wikipedia synthesis of the published accounts notes.[4] [12]
1832 1832-06 Paris, France First medals struck and distributed amid the cholera epidemic — After Fr Aladel obtained the authorization of Archbishop Hyacinthe-Louis de Quélen, the goldsmith Adrien Vachette strikes the first medals; the Daughters of Charity begin distributing them in June 1832, as a cholera epidemic kills over 20,000 in Paris. Details differ across sources: the chapel's site says 2,000 first medals distributed in June; the 1911 Catholic Encyclopedia dates the first striking 30 June 1832; Polistena's scholarly study records an order of 20,000 medals placed with Vachette in May 1832, delayed by the epidemic.[5] [8] [9]
1835 1835 ? Paris, France Mass diffusion — the people of Paris name the medal 'miraculous' — Reported cures and conversions drive explosive demand: the chapel's account counts over 500,000 medals by autumn 1834 and over a million worldwide in 1835; Polistena records some twenty million made in 1837 alone by Vachette and other Paris and Lyon goldsmiths. The popular renaming is recorded by the chapel's site: 'the people of Paris call the medal miraculous' — a popular attribution, not a canonical title.[5] [9]
1836 1836-02-16 ? Paris, France Canonical inquiry of the archdiocese of Paris opens — Archbishop de Quélen orders a diocesan investigation, directed by Vicar-General Quentin as promoter, opening (per the Vincentian-derived catechism used here) on 16 February 1836 and running through some nineteen sittings into July. It examined the seer's credibility (through Fr Aladel — Catherine refused to appear and stayed anonymous), the doctrinal content of the reported vision, the medal's extraordinary circulation (with the engraver's testimony), and attested cures and conversions. Its limits are contested between sources: devotional accounts (e.g. the Association of the Miraculous Medal) state the inquiry declared the medal of supernatural origin, while the detailed catechism account says the findings were favorable but the Archbishop pronounced no formal judgment and proceedings lapsed at his death in 1839; the French Wikipedia synthesis likewise notes no completed canonical trial ever declared the apparitions authentic.[10] [11] [12]
1840s
1842 1842-01-20 Rome, Italy Reported conversion of Alphonse Ratisbonne at Sant'Andrea delle Fratte — Alphonse Ratisbonne, a French Jew who had accepted to wear a Miraculous Medal, reported a vision of the Virgin 'as on the medal' in the church of Sant'Andrea delle Fratte in Rome, followed by his immediate conversion; he was baptized on 31 January 1842. A distinct reported event from the Paris apparitions, with its own Church inquiry (see June 1842).[14] [8]
1842 1842-06-03 Rome, Italy Roman canonical inquiry rules on the Ratisbonne conversion — After a canonical process before the tribunal of the Cardinal Vicar, Cardinal Costantino Patrizi, Vicar-General of Gregory XVI, issues a decree declaring Ratisbonne's instantaneous conversion 'a true and outstanding miracle' wrought by God through the intercession of the Blessed Virgin Mary, and permitting publication of the acts — a formal Church judgment on this Roman event, separate from any ruling on the Paris apparitions themselves.[14]
1870s
1876 1876-12-31 Paris, France Death of Catherine Labouré; her identity as the seer becomes known — Catherine Labouré dies at the Enghien hospice in Reuilly, Paris, where she had served the elderly poor for 46 years. Her identity as the seer of 1830, kept between her and her confessors for most of her life (the French Wikipedia notes the secret began to leak from about 1855, and she spoke to her superior shortly before dying), becomes publicly known around her death.[6] [12] [13]
1890s
1894 1894-07-23 Rome, Italy Leo XIII institutes the feast of the Miraculous Medal (27 November) — After examination of the facts by the Sacred Congregation of Rites, Leo XIII institutes a feast with proper Office and Mass of the Manifestation of the Immaculate Virgin under the title of the Miraculous Medal, celebrated yearly on 27 November — a liturgical act read by commentators as implicit Church approval of the devotion.[8] [12]
1930s
1933 1933-05-28 Rome, Italy Beatification of Catherine Labouré; her body exhumed the same year — Pius XI beatifies Catherine Labouré on 28 May 1933. Earlier that year her body was exhumed at Reuilly and reported found intact — a claim reported by devotional and encyclopedic sources; it now lies in a glass reliquary under a side altar of the rue du Bac chapel.[13] [11]
1940s
1947 1947-07-27 Rome, Italy Canonization of Catherine Labouré by Pius XII — Pius XII canonizes Catherine Labouré on 27 July 1947; addressing the pilgrims the next day (a speech published by the Holy See), he recalls the missions she attributed to the Virgin and the medal 'that the voice of the people did not hesitate to call miraculous'.[7] [13]

Key figures

Catherine Labouré

1806–1876 · France

Daughter of Charity; the reported seer of the 1830 rue du Bac apparitions. Remained publicly anonymous as the seer during her lifetime, serving the elderly poor at Reuilly for 46 years; beatified 1933, canonized 1947.[6] [13] [7]

Jean-Marie Aladel CM

1800–1865 · France

Vincentian priest, Catherine Labouré's confessor and spiritual director — the channel through which her account reached Church authorities; obtained Archbishop de Quélen's authorization and commissioned the medal from the goldsmith Vachette.[9] [8]

Hyacinthe-Louis de Quélen

1778–1839 · France

Archbishop of Paris (1821–1839). Authorized the striking of the medal (1832) and ordered the diocesan canonical inquiry of 1836; per the account used here he pronounced no formal judgment before his death in 1839.[9] [10]

Quentin

France

Vicar-General of Paris and promoter of the 1836 diocesan inquiry, which he directed through some nineteen sittings (per a devotional source drawing on 19th-century Vincentian accounts; biographical dates to verify).[10]

Adrien Vachette

1753–1839 · France

Paris goldsmith who received the order for the first medals in May 1832 and struck them from June 1832; his firm's sales figures document the medal's mass diffusion.[9]

Alphonse Ratisbonne

1814–1884 · France

French Jewish banker's son whose reported vision and instantaneous conversion at Sant'Andrea delle Fratte, Rome (20 January 1842) while wearing the medal was declared a miracle by the tribunal of the Cardinal Vicar in June 1842; later a Catholic priest.[14]

Costantino Patrizi Naro

1798–1876 · Italy

Cardinal Vicar of Rome under Gregory XVI; baptized Ratisbonne (31 January 1842) and signed the decree of 3 June 1842 declaring his conversion a miracle wrought through Mary's intercession.[14]

Related organizations

Daughters of Charity of Saint Vincent de Paul

Founded 1633 · Paris, France

The company Catherine Labouré belonged to; its motherhouse chapel at 140 rue du Bac is the site of the reported apparitions, and its sisters distributed the first medals in 1832. Not an enclosed order — a society of apostolic life serving the poor.[1] [5]

Congregation of the Mission (Vincentians / Lazarists)

Founded 1625 · Paris, France

Fr Aladel's congregation, which directs the Daughters of Charity spiritually; the 1894 feast was granted to it, and Vincentian houses worldwide propagate the devotion. Distinct from the Daughters of Charity, though both are of the Vincentian family.[8] [11]

Association of the Miraculous Medal

Perryville, Missouri, USA (US branch)

Vincentian-run devotional association propagating the medal; its historical account is cited here as a sympathetic, devotional source, not as an independent one.[11]

Disambiguation & nuance

Reported apparitions with partial and layered Church judgments are easily flattened into either 'approved miracle' or 'mere legend'. This project records who reported what and when, and what the Church ruled and when — nothing more.

Reported apparition ≠ Church-certified apparition

The 1830 rue du Bac apparitions are reported events, attested through Catherine Labouré via her confessor Aladel and her later written accounts. Church approval attached to the medal, the feast (1894) and the seer's canonization (1947); the French Wikipedia synthesis notes that no completed canonical trial ever formally declared the apparitions themselves authentic, while devotional sources describe the 1836 inquiry as recognizing the medal's supernatural origin. Both readings are recorded, attributed to their sources.[12] [11] [10]

Our Lady of Grace(s) ≠ one single devotion

In Brazil and the Hispanic world the medal's image is venerated as Nossa Senhora das Graças / Nuestra Señora de las Gracias, but 'Our Lady of Grace' also names older, unrelated devotions and images elsewhere. This chronology covers only the rue du Bac / Miraculous Medal devotion.[1] [8]

The Ratisbonne event (Rome, 1842) ≠ the rue du Bac apparitions (Paris, 1830)

Ratisbonne's reported vision at Sant'Andrea delle Fratte is a separate reported event, with its own canonical inquiry and its own formal decree (Cardinal Patrizi, 3 June 1842). Its approval is a judgment on that Roman conversion, not a canonical sentence on the Paris apparitions.[14]

'Miraculous' is a popular name, not a canonical verdict

The medal was struck in 1832 as the 'medal of the Immaculate Conception'; the chapel's own account records that it was the people of Paris who began calling it 'miraculous' amid reported cures and conversions. The name records popular devotion; each attributed miracle claim stands or falls on its own evidence and, where it exists, its own Church judgment.[5] [8]
